Looking back at a research career, a few milestones stand out as the ones that actually reshaped how I work. One of mine was in 2008, when I received a scholarship from the [Flemish Interuniversity Council for University Cooperation (VLIR-UOS)](https://www.vliruos.be/) to attend an international training programme at the Spatial Applications Division Leuven (SADL), [KU Leuven](https://www.kuleuven.be/english/), in Belgium.

Remote sensing and GIS were moving fast at the time, and the training at SADL was built around applying both to real land-evaluation and conservation problems rather than treating them as separate toolkits. I got to work through multi-spectral Landsat and early high-resolution imagery under the guidance of Prof. Dr. Ir. Jos Van Orshoven, who led SADL, and his critiques of how I was framing the analysis mattered as much as the technical content.

## Taking it home: Ghodaghodi Lake

The value of the fellowship showed up almost immediately after I got back. I used the processing approach I'd practiced in Leuven to analyse forest cover change around Ghodaghodi Lake, a Ramsar site in Kailali district, in Nepal's Far-Western Terai, resampling historical Multi-Spectral Scanner (MSS) data to a 30 m grid with cubic convolution, then geometrically correcting and histogram-matching it against 2.5 m ALOS pan-sharpened imagery as a control.

The resulting maps told a clear story: the northern hilly section of the area had stayed largely intact, while the southern plain forest was under real pressure, from population density, encroachment, and illegal cutting. That analysis became my paper, "Change assessment of forest cover in Ghodaghodi lake area in Kailali district of Nepal," published in [Banko Janakari](https://www.nepjol.info/index.php/BANKO) (full text on [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/250190406_Change_assessment_of_forest_cover_in_Ghodaghodi_lake_area_in_Kailali_district_of_Nepal)), and I acknowledged Prof. Van Orshoven's comments on the draft in the paper itself.

The workflows from that fellowship carried forward more than I expected at the time  they underpin the ALOS PALSAR biomass work I did with JAXA a few years later, and, in a roundabout way, the machine-learning carbon models I build now in the high Himalayas. Capacity-building programmes like the VLIR fellowship are often judged by the skills they teach, but for me the lasting part has been the network and the habit of scrutiny that came with it.
