### Abstract

The Central Himalayas represent one of the world's steepest environmental gradients and harbor some of the most carbon-dense forests on Earth, with aboveground biomass exceeding 1000 t ha⁻¹. Yet, global and regional forest carbon models systematically underestimate these exceptional carbon stocks, largely because they fail to capture fine-scale topoclimatic heterogeneity. Drawing from high-resolution climate downscaling and terrain analysis, this research quantified how microclimatic conditions shaped by slope, aspect, and curvature influence the spatial distribution of forest carbon in Nepal's mountain landscapes. Extreme carbon density occurs in distinct topographic settings that simultaneously optimize climatic energy and water availability, particularly in sheltered mid-elevation slopes with balanced radiation exposure and sufficient soil moisture. Conversely, areas prone to frequent geomorphic disturbances, such as avalanche tracks and steep convex slopes, exhibited significantly reduced biomass. Complementary work using regional forest inventory and geospatial models further revealed that topography mediates forest recovery and carbon accumulation following disturbance, underscoring the importance of local terrain-climate interactions in shaping carbon dynamics. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Himalayan forest carbon storage cannot be understood solely through coarse-scale climatic or vegetation models; rather, it emerges from the interplay between fine-scale topoclimatic gradients and landscape stability. This research contributes to a more mechanistic understanding of carbon distribution in mountainous ecosystems and provides a spatially explicit framework for conserving high-carbon landscapes under changing climatic and disturbance regimes.
